\section{Domain Overview}
\label{sec_domain_overview}

This chapter reviews eleven papers selected as the most directly relevant to the research questions established in Chapter~\ref{chapter_introduction}, identifies the structural patterns they share, and characterises the gaps that those questions address. Table~\ref{tab:lit-table} provides an overview; the sections that follow discuss each paper in depth.

Autonomous penetration testing --- the use of LLM agents to independently discover and exploit weaknesses in a target system without step-by-step human direction --- took shape as a distinct research area from approximately 2023, when \citet{deng2024pentestgpt} published the first systematic study of LLM-assisted penetration testing. Two established disciplines converge here: Vulnerability Assessment and Penetration Testing (VAPT), which relies on human operators following structured methodologies (reconnaissance, weakness identification, exploitation, and reporting), and LLM agent research, which studies how language models can plan, use tools, and act toward a goal across sequential steps. This is the setting RedGrid operates in: an LLM-driven multi-agent system designed to carry out the VAPT workflow autonomously against realistic, benchmarked targets.

Three capabilities define what an autonomous VAPT agent must exhibit. The first is \emph{exploration}: the agent must search a large space of candidate weaknesses across an unfamiliar attack surface without prior knowledge of which one is exploitable. CVE-Bench \citep{zhu2025cvebench} measures this directly and finds exploration failure rates of 37.5\%--80.0\% across every tested agent. The second is \emph{prerequisite reasoning}: many real exploits are not single actions but chains, where one weakness must precede a second before it becomes reachable. PentestEval \citep{yang2025pentesteval} measures this and finds that attack decision-making --- reasoning about which weakness to pursue next given what the agent has discovered --- is the weakest stage across nine evaluated LLMs, with an average Spearman correlation of 0.25 against expert judgement. The third is \emph{cross-surface generalisability}: whether the same architecture can operate effectively across qualitatively different attack surfaces (web applications, GraphQL APIs, multi-host networks) without surface-specific redesign. As the literature reviewed in this chapter shows, most systems address at most one of these three capabilities; none of the reviewed papers evaluates the same architecture across more than one attack-surface family.
