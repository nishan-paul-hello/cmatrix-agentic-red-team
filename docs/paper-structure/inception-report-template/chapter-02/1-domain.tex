\section{Domain Overview}
\label{sec_domain_overview}

This chapter reviews the recent literature on LLM-based autonomous penetration testing and identifies, at a preliminary level, the patterns and gaps that motivate the research direction described in Chapter~\ref{chapter_introduction}. The review covers a focused selection of eleven papers, chosen as the most directly relevant to our research question from a broader set of papers read during the preparation of this report. Table~\ref{tab:lit-table} provides an overview of these eleven papers; the sections that follow discuss them in more depth.

Autonomous penetration testing --- the use of LLM agents to independently discover and exploit weaknesses in a target system without step-by-step human direction --- has emerged as an active research area only in the last two to three years. The domain sits at the intersection of two older fields: Vulnerability Assessment and Penetration Testing (VAPT), which traditionally relies on human operators following structured methodologies (reconnaissance, weakness identification, exploitation, and reporting), and LLM agent research, which studies how language models can plan, use tools, and act over multiple steps toward a goal. Our work, RedGrid, is situated in this intersection: it is concerned with how an LLM-driven multi-agent system can carry out the VAPT workflow autonomously against realistic, benchmarked targets.

A useful way to frame the domain is around two capabilities that an autonomous VAPT agent must exhibit simultaneously. The first is \emph{exploration}: the agent must search a large space of candidate weaknesses across an unfamiliar attack surface without being told in advance which one is exploitable. The second is \emph{prerequisite reasoning}: many real exploits are not single actions but chains, where one weakness (for example, an information disclosure or a misconfigured endpoint) must be established before a second, more consequential weakness becomes reachable. As the literature reviewed in this chapter shows, most existing systems have been developed to strengthen one of these capabilities at the expense of the other --- a pattern that motivates the closer reading in the sections that follow.
